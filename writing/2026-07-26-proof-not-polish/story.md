Anybody can ship convincing software in 2026. The models write plausible code, the demos look considered, and every screenshot reads the same. What a screenshot cannot show is whether the numbers survive being checked.

I built a tool over the standard-charge files US hospitals are required by federal law to publish, starting with five Pittsburgh files: 4.5 million rows, two explicitly labeled snapshots. It answers questions about negotiated rates, and every answer carries its provenance: which hospitals and payers contributed rows, from which files, plus a hash that lets the exact question run again. The thing being served is a citable answer, not a dashboard.

The engineering that matters is invisible in that description, so here it is directly.

**Every published figure re-executes.** The findings on the homepage are generated by a deterministic sweep over the data. No model writes the prose; templates restate arithmetic, so a published sentence can always be traced to the calculation that produced it. Each finding stores its own query, and a tripwire re-runs every one of them against the live database: any dollar figure the sentence states must be a value the engine itself returned.

**The checks are layered, and each layer exists because the one above it passed on a wrong answer.** The first ties prose to engine output. The second ties engine output to real published rows: an interpolated median of two middle rates is nobody's price, so the corpus runs on the discrete median, a rate someone actually filed. The third asserts that the two rows being compared are the same kind of product, because a commercial rate and a Medicaid rate from the same insurer are prices of different things. The first two layers could not see a median crossing that line. Now the loader refuses to boot a corpus whose comparisons could cross it.

**The checks run against the live data, not fixtures.** The full suite executes the whole findings corpus against the real 4.5 million rows; the web suite adds 190 tests of its own. A bare `go test ./...` compiles none of the integration suite, and the Makefile says so at the top, because that silence once cost me a day of confident green that had never run the check this project exists for.

Now the part that is actually the point. A lot of this code was written by AI tooling, directed by me, and I am not precious about that. In 2026 this is not a confession; it is the job. The differentiating skill is not producing code. It is refusing to trust produced code.

That refusal has a shape, and the shape is the project: generated output never reaches a reader without passing a mechanical check; the corpus loader fails the boot rather than serve a finding it cannot validate; the sweep that writes findings is deterministic precisely so its output can be verified line by line; review, human or otherwise, is an input and never an authority. Language models are good at plausible. Plausible is not true. The system is built so the difference is enforced by code, not by my vigilance on a good day.

The receipts are public. The tool runs at [prices.georgelarson.me](https://prices.georgelarson.me), with the full corpus and its stored queries, the tripwires, and the record of what the checks caught: the first time the re-execution rule ran, twelve of twenty generated findings failed it. That list is its own post, linked from the tool.

None of this is theory for me. Seven years of my career were medical billing and EDI: payer portals, ACH transmission, X12 at a company that grew from about fifty people to national reach while I owned the integration framework. The domain taught me what a wrong number costs. The tooling taught me how fast wrong numbers can now be produced. This project is what I build when those two facts meet.

If you hire for healthcare data and want the augmented workflow with the verification bar attached, I am looking.
